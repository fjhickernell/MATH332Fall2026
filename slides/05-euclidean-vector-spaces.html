Points, Vectors, and Directions

The same coordinate column can play two different geometric roles

  • Why distinguish points from vectors?
  • The operations reveal the roles
  • Choosing an origin hides the distinction
  • Lines and planes combine points with directions

Why distinguish points from vectors?

Point

  • a location
  • written \(P,Q,\ldots\)
  • has no direction or length
  • depends on where the origin is placed when assigned coordinates

Vector

  • a displacement or direction
  • written \(\vct{u},\vct{v},\ldots\)
  • can be translated without changing it
  • has length and direction

\[ \overrightarrow{PQ}=Q-P \]

The distinction separates homogeneous direction spaces from translated solution sets

Points say where; vectors say how to move

Teaching purpose: coordinate notation makes points and vectors look identical, so students may miss why homogeneous solution sets are vector spaces while nonhomogeneous solution sets usually are not. Use the physical language first: a point is where you are, a vector tells you how to move, and the difference between two possible locations is a displacement. Then preview the algebraic payoff: differences between solutions of \(\mat{A}\vct{x}=\vct{b}\) solve the homogeneous system.

The operations reveal the roles

Operation Result Meaning
\(Q-P\) vector displacement from \(P\) to \(Q\)
\(P+\vct{v}\) point move from \(P\) by \(\vct{v}\)
\(\vct{u}+\vct{v}\) vector combine displacements
\(c\vct{v}\) vector scale or reverse a displacement
\(P+Q\) — no origin-independent geometric meaning

The expression

\[ (1-t)P+tQ=P+t(Q-P) \]

does have geometric meaning because its coefficients sum to \(1\)

Legal operations keep locations and directions from being confused

Choosing an origin hides the distinction

After choosing an origin \(O\), a point \(P\) receives the coordinate vector

\[ \vct{p}=\overrightarrow{OP} \]

Then both a point and a vector may be displayed as the same column:

\[ P\longleftrightarrow \vct{p}=\begin{bmatrix}p_1\\\vdots\\p_n\end{bmatrix} \]

Moving the origin changes the coordinates of \(P\)

The displacement between two points does not change:

\[ \overrightarrow{PQ}=\vct{q}-\vct{p} \]

Coordinates identify points with vectors only after an origin has been chosen

Lines and planes combine points with directions

A line needs one point and one nonzero direction:

\[ L=\{P+t\vct{v}:t\in\reals\} \]

A plane needs one point and two independent directions:

\[ \Pi=\{P+s\vct{u}+t\vct{v}:s,t\in\reals\} \]

Changing the point on the same line or plane does not change its directions

Only when the set passes through the origin can its points also be treated as a collection of vectors closed under addition and scaling

Point \(+\) directions describes a translated, or affine, set

Solution Sets Have Directions

This is why the point–vector distinction matters in linear algebra

  • Homogeneous systems produce directions
  • Nonhomogeneous systems translate those directions
  • Free variables count the directions
  • Read the affine structure from elimination

Homogeneous systems produce directions

The solutions of

\[ \mat{A}\vct{z}=\vct{0} \]

include \(\vct{0}\) and are closed under linear combinations:

\[ \mat{A}\vct{z}_1=\vct{0}, \quad \mat{A}\vct{z}_2=\vct{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mat{A}(c_1\vct{z}_1+c_2\vct{z}_2)=\vct{0} \]

These solutions are directions that the matrix cannot detect:

\[ \mat{A}(\vct{x}+\vct{z})=\mat{A}\vct{x} \]

These homogeneous directions form \(\operatorname{Null}(\mat{A})\); Deck 06 develops the null space as a subspace

Deck 06 will verify the vector-space axioms and connect the null space to the row and column spaces.

Nonhomogeneous systems translate those directions

Suppose \(\vct{x}_p\) is one particular solution of

\[ \mat{A}\vct{x}=\vct{b} \]

Every solution has the form

\[ \vct{x}=\vct{x}_p+\vct{z}, \qquad \mat{A}\vct{z}=\vct{0} \]

Conversely, the difference between any two solutions is homogeneous:

\[ \mat{A}\vct{x}_1=\mat{A}\vct{x}_2=\vct{b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mat{A}(\vct{x}_1-\vct{x}_2)=\vct{0} \]

Solution set \(=\) one solution point \(+\) all homogeneous directions

Free variables count the directions

For a consistent system with \(n\) unknowns

Free variables Solution geometry
\(0\) one point
\(1\) affine line
\(2\) affine plane
\(k\) \(k\)-parameter affine set

If there are \(r\) pivot variables, then there are

\[ n-r \]

free variables

The number of free parameters is the dimension of the homogeneous directions

This is a preview. Deck 06 will define basis and dimension; later work will name the relationship rank–nullity.

Read the affine structure from elimination

Consider

\[\begin{align*} x+y+z&=2\\ x-y+z&=0 \end{align*}\]

Elimination gives \(y=1\) and \(x+z=1\), hence

\[ \vct{x} =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{one solution point}} +t\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{homogeneous direction}} \]

\(\exstar\) Interpret this solution set Verify that the direction satisfies \(\mat{A}\vct{z}=\vct{0}\) Explain why adding \(t\vct{z}\) leaves both equations unchanged Decide whether the solution line passes through the origin

Euclidean Tools for Affine Questions

Reuse earlier geometry to compare points and construct directions

  • A special 3D operation: the cross product
  • Point differences build a plane normal
  • The triple product reconnects to determinants

Recall what the dot product already gives us

Deck 01 introduced the dot product as the geometry on \(\reals^n\)

We now use only these consequences:

\[\begin{align*} \vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}=0 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vct{u}\perp\vct{v}\\ \norm{\vct{v}} &=\sqrt{\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}}\\ \operatorname{dist}(P,Q) &=\norm{\vct{q}-\vct{p}} \end{align*}\]

Notice the roles in the last line:

\[ \underbrace{Q-P}_{\text{vector}} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \underbrace{\norm{\vct{q}-\vct{p}}}_{\text{distance between points}} \]

No new dot-product theory—only a tool for studying directions and differences

Projection finds the nearest point on an affine line

Let

\[ L=\{P+t\vct{v}:t\in\reals\}, \qquad \vct{v}\ne\vct{0} \]

To compare another point \(Q\) with \(L\), first form the displacement

\[ \overrightarrow{PQ}=\vct{q}-\vct{p} \]

Project that vector onto the line direction:

\[ \vct{x}_* =\vct{p} +\frac{(\vct{q}-\vct{p})^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}} {\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}}\vct{v} \]

The result \(\vct{x}_*\) represents the point of \(L\) nearest to \(Q\)

Subtract points, project the resulting vector, then add the displacement back to a point

A special 3D operation: the cross product

For \(\vct{u},\vct{v}\in\reals^3\), define

\[ \vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v} =\begin{bmatrix} u_2v_3-u_3v_2\\ u_3v_1-u_1v_3\\ u_1v_2-u_2v_1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The result is perpendicular to both inputs:

\[ \vct{u}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v})=0, \qquad \vct{v}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v})=0 \]

Its magnitude is parallelogram area, and reversing the inputs reverses its direction:

\[ \norm{\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v}} =\norm{\vct{u}}\norm{\vct{v}}\sin\theta, \qquad \vct{v}\mathbin{\times}\vct{u}=-(\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v}) \]

The ordinary cross product is a special operation on two vectors in \(\reals^3\)

The wedge product belongs to exterior algebra, a part of multilinear algebra that generalizes oriented areas, volumes, determinants, and the three-dimensional cross product.

The natural generalization in other dimensions is the exterior, or wedge, product. It records the oriented parallelogram determined by two vectors, but its output is a bivector rather than another ordinary vector. In three dimensions, perpendicularity identifies that oriented area with the vector \(\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v}\). There is also an exceptional vector cross product in dimension seven, but it is outside the scope of this course.

Point differences build a plane normal

For three noncollinear points \(P,Q,R\in\reals^3\), first form two directions:

\[ \vct{u}=\overrightarrow{PQ}=\vct{q}-\vct{p}, \qquad \vct{v}=\overrightarrow{PR}=\vct{r}-\vct{p} \]

Their cross product is a normal vector:

\[ \vct{n}=\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v}, \qquad \vct{n}^{\mathsf T}\vct{u} =\vct{n}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}=0 \]

The plane is therefore

\[ \vct{n}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{x}-\vct{p})=0 \]

The cross product is a useful three-dimensional construction, not part of the general vector-space structure

The triple product reconnects to determinants

For vectors \(\vct{u},\vct{v},\vct{w}\in\reals^3\),

\[ \vct{u}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{v}\mathbin{\times}\vct{w}) =\det\begin{bmatrix}\vct{u}&\vct{v}&\vct{w}\end{bmatrix} \]

  • absolute value — parallelepiped volume
  • sign — orientation of the ordered directions
  • zero — the directions lie in one plane

Point coordinates enter only through differences such as

\[ \overrightarrow{PQ},\quad \overrightarrow{PR},\quad \overrightarrow{PS} \]

Determinants measure the geometry of displacement vectors, independent of where the points are located

Big Ideas

  • Points are locations; vectors are displacements or directions
  • Choosing an origin lets both be represented by coordinate columns, but does not erase their different roles
  • Homogeneous solutions form a vector space of directions through the origin
  • A consistent nonhomogeneous solution set is one solution point plus all homogeneous directions
  • The dot product tests orthogonality and measures point differences
  • Cross products provide a special three-dimensional connection among point differences, normals, and determinants
