Vectors in \(\reals^n\)

Coordinates turn magnitude and direction into algebra

  • One vector has geometric and coordinate descriptions
  • Vector operations match geometric actions
  • The standard basis separates coordinate directions
  • Linear combinations build new directions
  • Vectors combine physical effects
  • Read geometry from coordinates

One vector has geometric and coordinate descriptions

Geometric vector

  • directed line segment
  • magnitude: how much
  • direction: which way
  • unchanged when translated without rotating or stretching

Coordinate vector

\[ \vct{v}=\begin{bmatrix}v_1\\v_2\\\vdots\\v_n\end{bmatrix} \in\reals^n \]

  • ordered list of components
  • represented as a column unless stated otherwise

If \(P=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\) and \(Q=(q_1,\ldots,q_n)\), then

\[ \overrightarrow{PQ}=\vct{q}-\vct{p} =\begin{bmatrix}q_1-p_1\\\vdots\\q_n-p_n\end{bmatrix} \]

Vector operations match geometric actions

For \(\vct{u},\vct{v}\in\reals^n\) and \(c\in\reals\)

\[ \vct{u}+\vct{v} =\begin{bmatrix}u_1+v_1\\\vdots\\u_n+v_n\end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\vct{v} =\begin{bmatrix}cv_1\\\vdots\\cv_n\end{bmatrix} \]

Addition

  • place vectors head to tail
  • parallelogram diagonal
  • combine displacements or effects

Scalar multiplication

  • \(c>0\): same direction, scaled length
  • \(c<0\): reversed direction, scaled length
  • \(c=0\): zero vector

Coordinate rules preserve the geometric meaning

The standard basis separates coordinate directions

The \(j\)th standard basis vector

\[ \vct{e}_j =\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0&1&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T} \]

Every coordinate vector decomposes uniquely:

\[ \vct{x}=x_1\vct{e}_1+\cdots+x_n\vct{e}_n \]

Example in \(\reals^3\)

\[ \begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\4\end{bmatrix} =2\vct{e}_1-\vct{e}_2+4\vct{e}_3 \]

Coordinates are the weights of the standard directions

Linear combinations build new directions

A linear combination of \(\vct{v}_1,\ldots,\vct{v}_k\) has the form

\[ c_1\vct{v}_1+\cdots+c_k\vct{v}_k \]

Two nonparallel vectors in \(\reals^2\) can build every vector in the plane

Two parallel vectors can build only vectors along one line

\(\exstar\) Can \(\vct{b}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix}\) be built from \(\vct{v}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vct{v}_2=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\)?

Translate the question into one matrix equation Interpret the solution coefficients geometrically

This revisits the column view of matrix multiplication. Formal span, linear independence, basis, and dimension belong to Deck 06.

Vectors combine physical effects

Two forces act on one object:

\[ \vct{F}_1=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}\text{ N}, \qquad \vct{F}_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{ N} \]

Resultant force

\[ \vct{F}=\vct{F}_1+\vct{F}_2 =\begin{bmatrix}2\\6\end{bmatrix}\text{ N} \]

The components answer algebraic questions; length and direction answer geometric questions

\[ \norm{\vct{F}}=\sqrt{40}=2\sqrt{10}\text{ N}, \qquad \theta=\operatorname{atan2}(6,2) \]

The same vector can be interpreted as displacement, velocity, force, data, or coefficients

Length, Distance, and Angle

The dot product converts two vectors into geometric information

  • The dot product measures alignment
  • The norm measures vector length
  • Distance is the length of a difference
  • Cauchy–Schwarz controls every dot product
  • The dot product determines angle
  • Cosine similarity ignores overall scale
  • Compare lengths, distances, and angles

The dot product measures alignment

Recall the standard dot product on \(\reals^n\):

\[ \vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v} =u_1v_1+\cdots+u_nv_n \]

It is

  • symmetric: \(\vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}=\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{u}\)
  • linear in each input
  • positive: \(\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}\ge0\)
  • definite: \(\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}=0\) only when \(\vct{v}=\vct{0}\)

For \(\vct{u}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vct{v}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\0\\2\end{bmatrix}\),

\[ \vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}=3+0-2=1 \]

Positive dot product — net alignment; negative — net opposition; zero — orthogonality

The norm measures vector length

The Euclidean norm

\[ \norm{\vct{v}} =\sqrt{\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}} =\sqrt{v_1^2+\cdots+v_n^2} \]

A unit vector has norm \(1\)

For \(\vct{v}\ne\vct{0}\), normalize without changing direction:

\[ \widehat{\vct{v}}=\frac{\vct{v}}{\norm{\vct{v}}}, \qquad \norm{\widehat{\vct{v}}}=1 \]

Norm properties

\[ \norm{\vct{v}}\ge0, \qquad \norm{c\vct{v}}=\abs{c}\norm{\vct{v}}, \qquad \norm{\vct{u}+\vct{v}}\le\norm{\vct{u}}+\norm{\vct{v}} \]

Distance is the length of a difference

For points represented by \(\vct{p},\vct{q}\in\reals^n\)

\[ \operatorname{dist}(\vct{p},\vct{q}) =\norm{\vct{q}-\vct{p}} =\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^n(q_j-p_j)^2} \]

Distance properties

\[\begin{align*} \operatorname{dist}(\vct{p},\vct{q})&\ge0 &\text{and equals zero exactly when }\vct{p}=\vct{q} \\ \operatorname{dist}(\vct{p},\vct{q})&=\operatorname{dist}(\vct{q},\vct{p}) &\text{symmetry} \\ \operatorname{dist}(\vct{p},\vct{r}) &\le \operatorname{dist}(\vct{p},\vct{q}) +\operatorname{dist}(\vct{q},\vct{r}) &\text{triangle inequality} \end{align*}\]

Subtract points to obtain a displacement vector; measure its norm to obtain distance

Cauchy–Schwarz controls every dot product

For all \(\vct{u},\vct{v}\in\reals^n\),

\[ \abs{\vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}} \le \norm{\vct{u}}\norm{\vct{v}} \]

Equality holds exactly when \(\vct{u}\) and \(\vct{v}\) are linearly dependent

Why the bound matters:

\[ -1\le \frac{\vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}} {\norm{\vct{u}}\norm{\vct{v}}} \le1 \qquad (\vct{u},\vct{v}\ne\vct{0}) \]

Cauchy–Schwarz makes the angle formula possible in every \(\reals^n\)

One proof considers the nonnegative quadratic \(\norm{\vct{u}-t\vct{v}}^2\) and requires its discriminant to be nonpositive. Develop that proof at the board if time permits.

The dot product determines angle

For nonzero vectors, define \(0\le\theta\le\pi\) by

\[ \cos\theta =\frac{\vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}} {\norm{\vct{u}}\norm{\vct{v}}} \]

Dot product Angle Geometry
\(>0\) \(0\le\theta<\pi/2\) acute; aligned
\(=0\) \(\theta=\pi/2\) orthogonal
\(<0\) \(\pi/2<\theta\le\pi\) obtuse; opposed

For unit vectors \(\widehat{\vct{u}}\) and \(\widehat{\vct{v}}\),

\[ \widehat{\vct{u}}^{\mathsf T}\widehat{\vct{v}}=\cos\theta \]

Cosine similarity ignores overall scale

For nonzero data vectors

\[ \operatorname{cosim}(\vct{x},\vct{y}) =\frac{\vct{x}^{\mathsf T}\vct{y}} {\norm{\vct{x}}\norm{\vct{y}}} \]

  • near \(1\): similar direction
  • near \(0\): weak directional relationship
  • near \(-1\): opposite direction

Multiplying either vector by a positive scalar leaves cosine similarity unchanged

Useful when

  • direction or profile matters
  • overall magnitude should be ignored

Not enough when

  • absolute size matters
  • offsets or unequal feature scales distort meaning

Geometry supplies a model; the application decides whether it is appropriate

Compare lengths, distances, and angles

Find \(\norm{\vct{u}}\), \(\norm{\vct{v}}\), and \(\norm{\vct{u}-\vct{v}}\) Find the angle between \(\vct{u}\) and \(\vct{v}\) Verify Cauchy–Schwarz numerically Explain which computation measures point separation and which measures directional similarity

Orthogonality and Projection

Split a vector into a chosen direction and a perpendicular residual

  • Orthogonality makes lengths add by squares
  • Projection extracts one directional component
  • Projection produces an orthogonal decomposition
  • The residual gives distance to a line
  • A projection is a matrix transformation
  • Complementary projections split every vector
  • Decompose a vector

Orthogonality makes lengths add by squares

Vectors \(\vct{u}\) and \(\vct{v}\) are orthogonal when

\[ \vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}=0 \]

Then

\[\begin{align*} \norm{\vct{u}+\vct{v}}^2 &=(\vct{u}+\vct{v})^{\mathsf T}(\vct{u}+\vct{v})\\ &=\norm{\vct{u}}^2+2\vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}+\norm{\vct{v}}^2\\ &=\norm{\vct{u}}^2+\norm{\vct{v}}^2 \end{align*}\]

The Pythagorean theorem is a dot-product identity

Projection extracts one directional component

Let \(\vct{v}\ne\vct{0}\)

The scalar component of \(\vct{u}\) along \(\vct{v}\) is

\[ \operatorname{comp}_{\vct{v}}\vct{u} =\frac{\vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}}{\norm{\vct{v}}} \]

The vector projection is

\[ \operatorname{proj}_{\vct{v}}\vct{u} =\frac{\vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}} {\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}}\vct{v} \]

If \(\widehat{\vct{v}}=\vct{v}/\norm{\vct{v}}\), then

\[ \operatorname{proj}_{\vct{v}}\vct{u} =(\widehat{\vct{v}}^{\mathsf T}\vct{u})\widehat{\vct{v}} \]

Projection produces an orthogonal decomposition

Write

\[ \vct{u}=\underbrace{\operatorname{proj}_{\vct{v}}\vct{u}}_{\text{parallel to }\vct{v}} +\underbrace{\vct{r}}_{\text{orthogonal to }\vct{v}} \]

where

\[ \vct{r}=\vct{u}-\operatorname{proj}_{\vct{v}}\vct{u} \]

Check orthogonality:

\[\begin{align*} \vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{r} &=\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{u} -\vct{v}^{\mathsf T} \left(\frac{\vct{u}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}} {\vct{v}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}}\vct{v}\right)\\ &=0 \end{align*}\]

Projection chooses the unique point on the line through \(\vct{v}\) with a perpendicular residual

The residual gives distance to a line

Line through \(\vct{x}_0\) with direction \(\vct{v}\ne\vct{0}\):

\[ L=\{\vct{x}_0+t\vct{v}:t\in\reals\} \]

From point \(\vct{p}\), first form the displacement \(\vct{p}-\vct{x}_0\)

Nearest point on the line

\[ \vct{x}_*=\vct{x}_0 +\operatorname{proj}_{\vct{v}}(\vct{p}-\vct{x}_0) \]

Distance to the line

\[ \operatorname{dist}(\vct{p},L) =\norm{\vct{p}-\vct{x}_*} \]

Nearest means perpendicular residual

A projection is a matrix transformation

For a unit vector \(\vct{q}\), projection onto its line is

\[ \operatorname{proj}_{\vct{q}}\vct{x} =(\vct{q}^{\mathsf T}\vct{x})\vct{q} =\underbrace{\vct{q}\vct{q}^{\mathsf T}}_{\mat{P}}\vct{x} \]

The projection matrix

\[ \mat{P}=\vct{q}\vct{q}^{\mathsf T} \]

satisfies

\[ \mat{P}^{\mathsf T}=\mat{P}, \qquad \mat{P}^2=\mat{P} \]

Symmetric — the geometry is orthogonal
Idempotent — projecting twice changes nothing

Complementary projections split every vector

Projection onto the line through unit \(\vct{q}\):

\[ \mat{P}=\vct{q}\vct{q}^{\mathsf T} \]

Projection onto the perpendicular hyperplane:

\[ \mat{Q}=\mat{I}-\mat{P} \]

Every \(\vct{x}\) splits as

\[ \vct{x}=\mat{P}\vct{x}+\mat{Q}\vct{x}, \qquad (\mat{P}\vct{x})^{\mathsf T}(\mat{Q}\vct{x})=0 \]

and therefore

\[ \norm{\vct{x}}^2 =\norm{\mat{P}\vct{x}}^2+\norm{\mat{Q}\vct{x}}^2 \]

Deck 03 constructed the plane projection \(\mat{Q}=\mat{I}-\vct{q}\vct{q}^{\mathsf T}\) from a unit normal vector. Here it appears as one half of a complementary pair.

Geometry of Linear Systems

Equations, parameters, and vectors describe the same solution set

  • A line is a point plus one direction
  • A plane has parametric and normal descriptions
  • A normal vector converts geometry into an equation
  • A linear system intersects hyperplanes
  • Every consistent solution set is a translate
  • Pivots and free variables predict geometry
  • Translate elimination into geometry

A line is a point plus one direction

A line through \(\vct{x}_0\in\reals^n\) parallel to \(\vct{v}\ne\vct{0}\):

\[ \vct{x}=\vct{x}_0+t\vct{v}, \qquad t\in\reals \]

In \(\reals^3\)

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\3\end{bmatrix} +t\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix} \]

is equivalent to

\[ x=1+2t, \qquad y=-2+t, \qquad z=3-t \]

Point \(+\) parameter \(\times\) direction

A plane has parametric and normal descriptions

Two directions

For nonparallel \(\vct{v},\vct{w}\in\reals^3\)

\[ \vct{x}=\vct{x}_0+s\vct{v}+t\vct{w} \]

\(s,t\in\reals\)

One normal

For \(\vct{n}\ne\vct{0}\) perpendicular to the plane

\[ \vct{n}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{x}-\vct{x}_0)=0 \]

equivalently \(\vct{n}^{\mathsf T}\vct{x}=b\)

The descriptions agree when

\[ \vct{n}^{\mathsf T}\vct{v}=0, \qquad \vct{n}^{\mathsf T}\vct{w}=0, \qquad b=\vct{n}^{\mathsf T}\vct{x}_0 \]

A normal vector converts geometry into an equation

Plane through \(\vct{x}_0=(1,0,2)\) with normal \(\vct{n}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{bmatrix}\)

Point-normal form

\[ \begin{bmatrix}2&-1&3\end{bmatrix} \left( \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{bmatrix} -\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{bmatrix} \right)=0 \]

Coordinate form

\[ 2(x-1)-y+3(z-2)=0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad 2x-y+3z=8 \]

Distance from \(\vct{p}\) to the plane

\[ \operatorname{dist}(\vct{p},H) =\frac{\abs{\vct{n}^{\mathsf T}\vct{p}-b}}{\norm{\vct{n}}} \]

A linear system intersects hyperplanes

Write the rows of \(\mat{A}\) as \(\vct{a}_1^{\mathsf T},\ldots, \vct{a}_m^{\mathsf T}\):

\[ \mat{A}\vct{x}=\vct{b} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \vct{a}_i^{\mathsf T}\vct{x}=b_i, \quad i=1,\ldots,m \]

Each equation describes a hyperplane with normal \(\vct{a}_i\)

The solution set is their intersection:

  • one point — unique solution
  • a line, plane, or higher-dimensional flat — infinitely many solutions
  • empty intersection — inconsistent system

Elimination finds the intersection; geometry explains its shape

Every consistent solution set is a translate

Suppose \(\vct{x}_p\) is one particular solution of \(\mat{A}\vct{x}=\vct{b}\)

Every other solution has the form

\[ \vct{x}=\vct{x}_p+\vct{z}, \qquad \mat{A}\vct{z}=\vct{0} \]

because

\[ \mat{A}(\vct{x}_p+\vct{z}) =\mat{A}\vct{x}_p+\mat{A}\vct{z} =\vct{b} \]

Conversely, the difference of any two solutions satisfies the homogeneous system

Consistent solution set \(=\) one particular point \(+\) all homogeneous directions

Deck 06 will name the homogeneous directions the null space and explain why they form a subspace.

Pivots and free variables predict geometry

For a consistent system with \(n\) unknowns

Free variables Typical solution geometry in \(\reals^n\)
\(0\) one point
\(1\) line
\(2\) plane
\(k\) \(k\)-parameter flat

If there are \(r\) pivot variables, then there are

\[ n-r \]

free variables

The number of free parameters is the dimension of the solution directions

State this as geometric preview. Rank, nullity, and the fundamental spaces are developed formally in Deck 06.

Translate elimination into geometry

Consider

\[\begin{align*} x+y+z&=2\\ x-y+z&=0 \end{align*}\]

Elimination gives \(y=1\) and \(x+z=1\)

\[ \vct{x} =\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix} +t\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

The direction vector is perpendicular to both row normals:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T} \begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}=0, \qquad \begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T} \begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}=0 \]

Cross Products and Orientation

In \(\reals^3\), two directions determine a perpendicular direction and an area

  • The cross product produces a perpendicular vector
  • Order determines orientation
  • Cross-product magnitude is parallelogram area
  • The scalar triple product is signed volume
  • Cross products build plane normals
  • Torque combines leverage and force
  • Use the cross product geometrically

The cross product produces a perpendicular vector

For \(\vct{u},\vct{v}\in\reals^3\)

\[ \vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v} =\begin{bmatrix} u_2v_3-u_3v_2\\ u_3v_1-u_1v_3\\ u_1v_2-u_2v_1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Its defining geometry:

\[ \vct{u}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v})=0, \qquad \vct{v}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v})=0 \]

For \(\vct{e}_1\) and \(\vct{e}_2\),

\[ \vct{e}_1\mathbin{\times}\vct{e}_2=\vct{e}_3 \]

Two input vectors in \(\reals^3\) produce a vector perpendicular to both

Order determines orientation

The cross product is not commutative:

\[ \vct{v}\mathbin{\times}\vct{u} =-(\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v}) \]

Right-hand rule

  • curl fingers from \(\vct{u}\) toward \(\vct{v}\) through the smaller angle
  • thumb points along \(\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v}\)

Cyclic standard-basis products

\[ \vct{e}_1\mathbin{\times}\vct{e}_2=\vct{e}_3, \qquad \vct{e}_2\mathbin{\times}\vct{e}_3=\vct{e}_1, \qquad \vct{e}_3\mathbin{\times}\vct{e}_1=\vct{e}_2 \]

Reverse either order to reverse the sign

Cross-product magnitude is parallelogram area

If \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vct{u}\) and \(\vct{v}\), then

\[ \norm{\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v}} =\norm{\vct{u}}\norm{\vct{v}}\sin\theta \]

Base times perpendicular height

\[ \underbrace{\norm{\vct{u}}}_{\text{base}} \underbrace{\norm{\vct{v}}\sin\theta}_{\text{height}} \]

Triangle area

\[ \frac12\norm{\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v}} \]

The area is zero exactly when the vectors are parallel or one is zero

Dot product measures alignment; cross-product magnitude measures perpendicular spread

The scalar triple product is signed volume

For \(\vct{u},\vct{v},\vct{w}\in\reals^3\)

\[ \vct{u}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{v}\mathbin{\times}\vct{w}) =\det\begin{bmatrix}\vct{u}&\vct{v}&\vct{w}\end{bmatrix} \]

Absolute value — volume of the parallelepiped

Sign — orientation of the ordered triple

Zero — the three vectors lie in one plane

The cross product reconnects Chapter 3 geometry to the determinant

Cross products build plane normals

Three noncollinear points \(P,Q,R\in\reals^3\) determine a plane

Two directions in the plane:

\[ \vct{u}=\overrightarrow{PQ}, \qquad \vct{v}=\overrightarrow{PR} \]

A normal vector:

\[ \vct{n}=\vct{u}\mathbin{\times}\vct{v} \]

Plane equation:

\[ \vct{n}^{\mathsf T}(\vct{x}-\vct{p})=0 \]

Two in-plane directions \(\longrightarrow\) one perpendicular normal \(\longrightarrow\) one plane equation

Torque combines leverage and force

Force \(\vct{F}\) applied at position \(\vct{r}\) relative to a pivot produces torque

\[ \vct{\tau}=\vct{r}\mathbin{\times}\vct{F} \]

Magnitude

\[ \norm{\vct{\tau}} =\norm{\vct{r}}\norm{\vct{F}}\sin\theta \]

  • only the component of force perpendicular to the lever arm turns the object
  • direction gives the rotation axis by the right-hand rule
  • zero torque when force acts along the lever arm

Cross products package size and oriented perpendicular direction together

Use the cross product geometrically

\(\exstar\) Let \(P=(1,0,0)\), \(Q=(2,1,0)\), and \(R=(0,1,2)\)

Find \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\mathbin{\times}\overrightarrow{PR}\) Find the area of triangle \(PQR\) Find an equation of the plane through \(P,Q,R\) Explain what changes when \(Q\) and \(R\) are interchanged

Big Ideas

  • A vector in \(\reals^n\) is both a coordinate object and a geometric direction
  • Dot products create length, distance, angle, and orthogonality
  • Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality guarantee that these measurements behave geometrically
  • Projection splits a vector into parallel and perpendicular components; the residual measures nearest-point error
  • A consistent linear system describes one particular point plus all homogeneous directions
  • In \(\reals^3\), the cross product supplies an oriented perpendicular direction, area, plane normals, and volume
