Preserved Directions

Which lines through the origin does a matrix send into themselves?

  • An eigenvector stays on its own line
  • Chosen directions reveal the action

An eigenvector stays on its own line

For a square matrix \(\mat{A}\), an eigenvector is a nonzero vector \(\vct{v}\) satisfying

\[ \mat{A}\vct{v}=\lambda\vct{v},\qquad \vct{v}\ne\vct{0} \]

The scalar \(\lambda\) is its eigenvalue

  • Every multiple of \(\vct{v}\) is mapped into its span

\[\begin{equation*} \mat{A}\operatorname{span}\{\vct{v}\}\subseteq\operatorname{span}\{\vct{v}\}, \qquad \text{equality when }\lambda\ne0 \end{equation*}\]

  • A nonzero multiple is another eigenvector for the same eigenvalue
  • The zero vector satisfies the equation for every \(\lambda\), so it is excluded

The line is preserved as a subspace; its vectors may stretch, reverse, or collapse

A is n-by-n over a specified field, initially R. For any scalar c, A(cv)=cAv=lambda(cv). When lambda=0 the entire line maps to zero, so “preserved” means invariant (mapped into itself), not necessarily mapped onto itself. A nonzero eigenvector can have zero entries. Zero is an allowed eigenvalue, but the zero vector is never an eigenvector. The real/complex field distinction becomes essential in the complex-eigenvalue section.

Chosen directions reveal the action

\[ \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \vct{v}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad \vct{v}_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix} \]

\[ \mat{A}\vct{v}_1=3\vct{v}_1,\qquad \mat{A}\vct{v}_2=\vct{v}_2 \]

The standard axes mix; the two diagonal directions act independently

An eigenvalue describes a scalar action

For a real eigenvalue and its eigenvector:

Eigenvalue Action on that vector
\(\lambda>1\) stretch in the same direction
\(0<\lambda<1\) shrink in the same direction
\(\lambda=1\) leave fixed
\(\lambda<0\) reverse direction and scale length by \(|\lambda|\)
\(\lambda=0\) send to zero

For a square matrix,

\[ 0\text{ is an eigenvalue}\iff\operatorname{null}(\mat{A})\ne\{\vct{0}\} \iff\mat{A}\text{ is singular} \]

The scale factor belongs to a direction; it need not describe every vector

A nonzero null vector identifies an eigenvalue

For \(\mat{A}\in\reals^{n\times n}\) and \(\vct{v}\ne\vct{0}\):

\[ \mat{A}\vct{v}=\lambda\vct{v} \iff(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I}_n)\vct{v}=\vct{0} \iff\vct{0}\ne\vct{v}\in\nullspace(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I}_n) \]

A nonzero solution exists exactly when the shifted matrix is singular:

\[ \det(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I}_n)=0 \]

Define the characteristic polynomial

\[ p_{\mat{A}}(\lambda)=\det(\lambda\mat{I}_n-\mat{A}) \]

Some sources use \(\det(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I}_n)\) — the same roots and multiplicities

First solve the scalar polynomial equation; then solve a homogeneous system for each root

We follow Anton’s monic convention det(lambda I-A), with leading coefficient 1 and constant term (-1)^n det(A). The alternative det(A-lambda I) differs by (-1)^n and has exactly the same roots with the same multiplicities. The two polynomials agree in even dimensions and have opposite signs in odd dimensions. A characteristic equation may be multiplied by a nonzero constant without changing its solutions. The equivalence uses the invertibility/determinant results already taught. To find eigenvectors after finding a root, reduce A-lambda I and solve its homogeneous system. A root guarantees at least one free variable, not a unique eigenvector. Never divide a vector equation by a vector.

Example: find eigenvalues, then eigenvectors

Return to \(\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\)

\[ p_{\mat{A}}(\lambda) =\det\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-2&-1\\-1&\lambda-2\end{bmatrix} =(\lambda-2)^2-1=(\lambda-3)(\lambda-1) \]

For each eigenvalue, solve a different homogeneous system:

\(\lambda\) \(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I}\) Solution condition Eigenspace basis
\(3\) \(\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\) \(v_2=v_1\) \(\{[1,1]^\top\}\)
\(1\) \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\) \(v_2=-v_1\) \(\{[1,-1]^\top\}\)

The determinant finds the scale factors; null spaces find their directions

An eigenspace includes all vectors for one eigenvalue

For an eigenvalue \(\lambda\), its eigenspace is

\[ E_\lambda=\operatorname{null}(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I}) \]

  • It is a subspace: zero and all linear combinations remain in it
  • Its nonzero vectors are exactly the eigenvectors with eigenvalue \(\lambda\)
  • Its basis comes from free variables in the shifted system

By rank–nullity,

\[ \dim(E_\lambda)=n-\operatorname{rank}(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I})\ge1 \]

Eigenvectors exclude zero; the eigenspace includes zero

Example: a spinning tennis racket

The inertia tensor \(\mat{J}=\mat{J}^\top\in\reals^{3\times3}\) describes rotational inertia about the center of mass

\[\begin{equation*} \mat{J}\vct{v}_j=I_j\vct{v}_j,\qquad 0<I_1<I_2<I_3 \end{equation*}\]

  • Principal-axis directions \(\vct{v}_j\): eigenvectors; principal moments \(I_j\): eigenvalues
  • Each eigenspace is a line: \(E_{I_j}=\operatorname{span}\{\vct{v}_j\}\)
Axis through the center of mass Moment Rotation without external torque
Along the handle Smallest Stable
Across the racket, in its plane Intermediate Unstable
Perpendicular to the racket face Largest Stable

Rotation about the intermediate axis is possible, but small disturbances grow and make the racket tumble

Demonstration: mark one face of the racket, compare tosses about the three principal axes, and watch the extra face flip for the intermediate-axis toss. Treat the racket as a rigid, approximately symmetric flat body. All axes pass through its center of mass, and external torque about that point is neglected. The tensor maps angular velocity to angular momentum; principal-axis rotation makes those vectors parallel. The tensor is not the matrix of a spatial rotation. Its three distinct principal moments make these eigenspaces one-dimensional. Exact steady rotation about the intermediate axis is a valid motion; instability means small off-axis disturbances grow, not that the motion is impossible. The stability assertion is a result of rigid-body dynamics, not a consequence of the eigenvalue definition alone.

Sources: UT Austin: principal axes of rotation and Harvard: tennis racquet flip.

A small wobble may oscillate or grow

Perfect spin about any principal axis is possible — test it with a tiny wobble

Let \(\vct{u}\) contain the two small angular-velocity components perpendicular to the spin axis

The linear approximation uses a different matrix, \(\mat{B}\)

\[ \vct{u}'=\mat{B}\vct{u},\qquad \mat{B}\vct{w}=\mu\vct{w} \implies \vct{u}(t)=e^{\mu t}\vct{w} \]

Steady spin axis Wobble eigenvalues Effect of a small disturbance
Smallest or largest moment \(\pm\sqrt{-1}\,\nu\) Oscillation; wobble stays small
Intermediate moment \(\pm\sigma\) A growing component; onset of tumbling

\(\nu,\sigma>0\) — imaginary rates oscillate; a positive real rate grows initially

Inertia eigenvalues are principal moments; wobble eigenvalues govern growth or oscillation

Keep the distinction verbal and brief: the inertia eigenvectors are the natural spin axes, and the three positive inertia eigenvalues are the principal moments. Perfect torque-free spin about any one axis is possible. The new question is whether a tiny off-axis wobble stays small. The angular velocity components are measured in the body-fixed principal-axis frame. B is the two-dimensional transverse linearization about that chosen steady spin, not the inertia tensor J. An eigenvector mode in the linear model evolves as exp(mu t)w; its real and imaginary parts give real oscillatory solutions when mu is purely imaginary. The displayed mode has initial data w, and general small disturbances combine the two modes. For the smallest and largest moments the pair is purely imaginary, giving neutral oscillation, not decay back to the exact spin. For the middle moment there is one positive and one negative real rate; a generic perturbation contains the growing mode. The full three-component angular-velocity linearization also has a zero eigenvalue, corresponding to changing the steady spin speed along the same axis. It is omitted because u contains only the two perpendicular components. Assume a rigid body, no external torque, three distinct principal moments and nonzero spin. Conserved energy and angular-momentum magnitude support the bounded-wobble interpretation at the extreme axes; imaginary eigenvalues alone do not prove nonlinear stability for every system. Intermediate-axis growth describes the initial small disturbance; the full motion remains energy bounded and develops into tumbling rather than unbounded spin speed. Optional physical intuition: at fixed angular-momentum magnitude, the energy has extrema at the extreme-moment axes and a saddle at the middle axis. Nearby conserved-energy contours surround the extrema but allow departures near the saddle. No Euler-equation derivation is needed for this illustration.

Source: TU Delft: Euler’s equations and the tennis racket theorem.

Compute and check an eigenspace

\[ \mat{B}=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix} \]

\(\exstar\) Use determinants, homogeneous systems, and direct multiplication Find the characteristic polynomial and both eigenvalues Find a basis for each eigenspace Check \(\mat{B}\vct{v}=\lambda\vct{v}\) for each chosen basis vector Does \(\vct{e}_2\) have to be an eigenvector because \(\mat{B}\) is triangular?

p_B=(lambda-4)(lambda-2). E_4=span{[1,0]^T}; E_2=span{[1,-2]^T}. B[1,-2]T=[2,-4]T=2[1,-2]^T. B e2=[1,2]^T, not a multiple of e2. Triangular diagonal entries give eigenvalues, not necessarily standard-basis eigenvectors. The two chosen eigenvectors are independent.

Trace and determinant check the eigenvalues

Count all \(n\) eigenvalues with algebraic multiplicity, allowing complex roots

\[ \operatorname{tr}(\mat{A})=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j, \qquad \det(\mat{A})=\prod_{j=1}^n\lambda_j \]

For \(\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\): \(\quad3+1=4\), \(\quad3\cdot1=3\)

For any triangular matrix, the characteristic determinant is a product:

\[ p_{\mat{U}}(\lambda)=\prod_{j=1}^n(\lambda-u_{jj}) \]

Triangular eigenvalues are the diagonal entries; trace and determinant are checks, not a general eigensolver

The coefficient of lambda^(n-1) in the monic characteristic polynomial is -tr(A); its constant coefficient is (-1)^n det(A). Comparing with the factored polynomial proves both identities, without diagonalizability. The identities do not generally determine all eigenvalues when n>2. For a triangular matrix, lambda I-U remains triangular, so its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries. A real matrix may have nonreal roots; that topic is developed below rather than excluded by the real entries.

Use software for practical eigenvalue problems

Hand calculations reveal the ideas; software handles practical matrix sizes

  • Diagonal or triangular matrices: read eigenvalues from the diagonal
  • General matrices: use an eigenvalue solver rather than expand a characteristic determinant
  • Python: numpy.linalg.eig(A); for real symmetric matrices, numpy.linalg.eigh(A)

For a computed eigenpair, check

\[\begin{equation*} \mat{A}\widehat{\vct{v}}\approx\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{\vct{v}}, \qquad \widehat{\vct{v}}\ne\vct{0} \end{equation*}\]

Understand the eigenvalue equation and interpret the directions; let software do the larger computations

The characteristic polynomial defines the eigenvalues, but numerical eigensolvers normally work directly with the matrix rather than expand that polynomial and find its roots. Use small or structured examples for exact hand calculations. Once an exact eigenvalue is known, row reduction of A-lambda I finds its eigenspace. Row reduction of A itself does not preserve its eigenvalues. Numerical eigenvectors are approximate and may be rescaled or reordered; check the corresponding eigenvalue/vector pair together. Interpret the residual relative to the scales of A and v. A small residual alone does not guarantee small forward errors for a sensitive nonsymmetric eigenvalue problem. The companion demonstrates eigh, residual checks and orthonormality.

Eigenvector Bases and Diagonalization

When do preserved directions provide enough coordinates for every vector?

  • An eigenvector basis makes the matrix diagonal
  • Repeated roots may leave too few directions
  • A diagonalization recipe
  • Optional: generalized eigenvectors
  • Similarity changes coordinates, not the operator

An eigenvector basis makes the matrix diagonal

For the ordered eigenbasis \(\mathcal{V}=\{\vct{v}_1,\ldots,\vct{v}_n\}\), match each column to its eigenvalue

\[ \mat{V}=[\vct{v}_1\ \cdots\ \vct{v}_n],\qquad \mat{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n) \]

Multiplication by columns gives

\[ \mat{A}\mat{V}=[\lambda_1\vct{v}_1\ \cdots\ \lambda_n\vct{v}_n] =\mat{V}\mat{D} \]

Independence makes \(\mat{V}\) invertible:

\[ \boxed{\mat{V}^{-1}\mat{A}\mat{V}=\mat{D}},\qquad \mat{A}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\mat{V}^{-1} \]

\(\mat{A}\) is diagonalizable over the chosen field exactly when it has an eigenvector basis there

The converse is equally important. If V^{-1} A V=D is diagonal and V is invertible, multiply by V to obtain AV=VD. Column j says A v_j=lambda_j v_j. The columns of invertible V form a basis, so this is an eigenvector basis. The order of eigenvalues must match the order of columns. Scaling a column by a nonzero constant gives another valid V with the same D. A matrix with an eigenvalue zero may still be diagonalizable; V, not D, must be invertible.

Example: diagonalize and read the coordinates

For \(\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\), choose

\[ \mat{V}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mat{V}^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mat{D}=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

For \(\vct{x}=[4,2]^\top\), write \(\vct{c}=[\vct{x}]_{\mathcal{V}}=\mat{V}^{-1}\vct{x}\)

\[ \mat{A}\vct{x}=\begin{bmatrix}10\\8\end{bmatrix} \quad\xleftarrow{\mat{V}}\quad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}9\\1\end{bmatrix}}_{[\mat{A}\vct{x}]_{\mathcal{V}}} \quad\xleftarrow{\mat{D}}\quad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}}_{[\vct{x}]_{\mathcal{V}}} \]

Read coordinates, scale them separately, then reconstruct the output

Check V D V^{-1}=A and V^{-1}V=I. The input x is 3v1+v2; the output is 9v1+v2. Direct multiplication gives [10,8]^T. V maps eigenbasis coordinates to standard coordinates; V^{-1} goes the reverse way. This is the same coordinate route taught in Bases and Coordinates.

Diagonalization is a change of coordinates

Use the inverse basis matrix as in Bases and Coordinates

Eigenbasis coordinates \([\vct{x}]_{\mathcal{V}}=\vct{c}=\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\\vdots\\c_n\end{bmatrix}\)

\(\xleftarrow{\quad\mat{V}\quad}\)

Vector \(\displaystyle\vct{x}=\sum_{j=1}^n c_j\vct{v}_j\) \(\vct{x}=\mat{V}\vct{c}\)

Apply \(\mat{A}\) to the vector or \(\mat{D}\) to its eigenbasis coordinates

\[ \mat{A}\vct{x}=\mat{A}(\mat{V}\vct{c})=\mat{V}(\mat{D}\vct{c}), \qquad [\mat{A}\vct{x}]_{\mathcal{V}}=\mat{D}[\vct{x}]_{\mathcal{V}} \]

\(\mat{V}^{-1}\) reads the coordinates; \(\mat{D}\) scales them separately

The operator is unchanged; the new coordinates separate its scalar actions

Recall the coordinate-to-vector boxes from Bases and Coordinates. The right box contains the eigenbasis coordinate column; the left box contains the vector described in standard coordinates. The scalar field is real or complex, as specified for the chosen eigenbasis. AV=VD says that building the vector then applying A agrees with applying D to its coordinates then building the output vector. The basis may be oblique or scaled; V need not preserve lengths or angles. For the preceding example, c=[3,1]^T and Dc=[9,1]^T; V sends these to x=[4,2]^T and Ax=[10,8]^T. Later replace the single action D by e^(tD): the same coordinate change will separate an entire differential system.

Distinct eigenvalues supply independent eigenvectors

Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent

For two eigenvectors, suppose \(c_1\vct{v}_1+c_2\vct{v}_2=\vct{0}\)

Apply \(\mat{A}\), then substitute \(c_2\vct{v}_2=-c_1\vct{v}_1\) from the original relation

\[\begin{align*} \vct{0}&=c_1\lambda_1\vct{v}_1+\lambda_2(c_2\vct{v}_2)\\ &=c_1\lambda_1\vct{v}_1-\lambda_2 c_1\vct{v}_1\\ &=c_1(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\vct{v}_1 \end{align*}\]

Since \(\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2\) and \(\vct{v}_1\ne\vct{0}\), \(c_1=0\), then \(c_2=0\)

For \(k\) eigenvectors, eliminate the last term as above and apply induction to the first \(k-1\)

An \(n\times n\) matrix with \(n\) distinct eigenvalues in the chosen field is diagonalizable there

The one-vector case holds because an eigenvector is nonzero. For k distinct eigenvalues, start with a zero relation among v1,…,vk, apply A, and use the original relation to substitute for c_k v_k in the term lambda_k(c_k v_k). The remaining k-1 terms have coefficients c_j(lambda_j-lambda_k). By induction the first k-1 vectors are independent, so those coefficients vanish. Distinct eigenvalues give c_j=0 for j<k. The original relation then gives c_k=0 since v_k is nonzero. The identity matrix shows that diagonalizability can also hold with repeated eigenvalues.

Repeated roots permit diagonalization, but do not guarantee enough eigenvectors

Count eigenspace dimensions before building a basis

For each eigenvalue \(\lambda\):

Multiplicity What it counts
Algebraic, \(a_\lambda\) repetitions of the root in \(p_{\mat{A}}\)
Geometric, \(g_\lambda\) \(\dim(E_\lambda)\): independent eigenvectors available

\[ 1\le g_\lambda\le a_\lambda \]

Over a field in which \(p_{\mat{A}}\) splits into linear factors:

\[ \mat{A}\text{ diagonalizable} \iff\sum_{\text{distinct }\lambda}g_\lambda=n \iff g_\lambda=a_\lambda\text{ for every }\lambda \]

Collect a basis of each eigenspace; diagonalization needs \(n\) independent columns altogether

For a real eigenbasis all roots must be real. Over C the characteristic polynomial always splits. To justify g<=a, extend a basis of E_lambda to one of the whole space. In that basis A has block form [[lambda I_g, B],[0,C]], whose characteristic polynomial contains (t-lambda)^g. Thus g<=a. Eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues form a direct sum: apply the distinct-eigenvalue independence argument to a relation among nonzero vectors, one from each eigenspace. Combining bases of the individual eigenspaces is therefore independent. Since sum a=n when the polynomial splits, the three displayed conditions are equivalent. A repeated root is a prompt to compute nullity, not evidence of failure.

A diagonalization recipe

For a square matrix over the chosen field:

  1. Factor \(p_{\mat{A}}(\lambda)\) and list its distinct roots and multiplicities
  2. For each root, solve \((\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I})\vct{v}=\vct{0}\) and find an eigenspace basis
  3. Count all independent eigenvectors obtained; fewer than \(n\) means no eigenvector basis
  4. If there are \(n\), put them into \(\mat{V}\) and their matching eigenvalues into \(\mat{D}\)
  5. Check \(\mat{A}\mat{V}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\) and that \(\mat{V}\) is invertible

If the characteristic polynomial does not split in the field, diagonalization there is impossible

 

Verification by \(\mat{A}\mat{V}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\) avoids computing an inverse

Optional: generalized eigenvectors

Optional enrichment; may be skipped

Let \(\lambda\) be a characteristic root of algebraic multiplicity \(k\)

Its generalized eigenspace is

\[ G_\lambda=\nullspace\bigl((\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I}_n)^k\bigr),\qquad E_\lambda=\nullspace(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I}_n)\subseteq G_\lambda \]

A generalized eigenvector is any nonzero vector in \(G_\lambda\)

Ordinary eigenvectors are included; a new direction lies in \(G_\lambda\setminus E_\lambda\)

A length-two chain satisfies

\[ (\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I})\vct{v}=\vct{0},\qquad (\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I})\vct{w}=\vct{v}\ne\vct{0} \]

\(\mat{A}\vct{v}=\lambda\vct{v}\), but \(\mat{A}\vct{w}=\lambda\vct{w}+\vct{v}\)

The second direction feeds into the first instead of scaling independently

This is optional enrichment, not a commitment to a Jordan algorithm. Here k is the algebraic multiplicity of this eigenvalue: it is a sufficient power for the whole generalized eigenspace, not necessarily the smallest power that kills a particular vector. The original definition using some positive integer power is equivalent to membership in this G_lambda. Ordinary eigenvectors satisfy every positive-power condition; taking a power greater than one alone does not exclude them. New generalized directions are the vectors in G_lambda outside E_lambda. In the displayed length-two chain, w is not an ordinary eigenvector because (A-lambda I)w is nonzero, but (A-lambda I)^2 w=0. The vectors v,w are independent: apply A-lambda I to av+bw=0 to obtain bv=0, hence b=0 and then a=0. Use G for this generalized basis, V for a basis of ordinary eigenvectors, and Q for an orthonormal eigenvector matrix. The basis-existence statement below is a theorem; no general Jordan algorithm is required for the example.

A chain supplies the missing second direction

Return to the matrix with only one independent eigenvector:

\[ \mat{J}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \vct{v}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad \vct{w}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix} \]

\[ (\mat{J}-2\mat{I})\vct{v}=\vct{0},\qquad (\mat{J}-2\mat{I})\vct{w}=\vct{v},\qquad (\mat{J}-2\mat{I})^2\vct{w}=\vct{0} \]

The two vectors form a basis \(\mat{G}=[\vct{v}\ \vct{w}]\), with

\[ \mat{J}\mat{G} =\mat{G}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{Jordan block}} \]

The missing eigenvector is replaced by a generalized eigenvector

The off-diagonal \(1\) records the coupling; the matrix is still not diagonalizable

Here G=I because J is already a Jordan block. The basis still illustrates the distinction: its second column is generalized, not an ordinary eigenvector. A vector av+bw is sent to (2a+b)v+2bw. Thus the diagonal entries scale coefficients while the off-diagonal entry mixes them. The nonzero off-diagonal entry cannot be removed by choosing a different ordinary eigenvector basis, because the eigenspace has dimension one.

Generalized eigenspaces supply a full basis

For \(\mat{A}\in\reals^{n\times n}\), suppose all characteristic roots are real, with distinct roots \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_s\) and algebraic multiplicities \(k_1,\ldots,k_s\)

\[ G_{\lambda_j}=\nullspace\bigl((\mat{A}-\lambda_j\mat{I}_n)^{k_j}\bigr), \qquad\dim G_{\lambda_j}=k_j,\qquad\sum_{j=1}^s k_j=n \]

Choose a basis of each \(G_{\lambda_j}\) and combine the lists

The result is \(n\) independent generalized eigenvectors \(\vct{g}_1,\ldots,\vct{g}_n\):

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{span}\{\vct{g}_1,\ldots,\vct{g}_n\}=\reals^n} \]

A generalized eigenvector basis gives coordinates even when an ordinary eigenvector basis is missing

With nonreal roots, work over \(\mathbb{C}\) instead: the span is \(\mathbb{C}^n\)

State the generalized-eigenspace decomposition as a theorem. When the characteristic polynomial splits over the scalar field, each generalized eigenspace has dimension equal to the corresponding algebraic multiplicity, and the generalized eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues form a direct sum of the whole space. Hence their individual bases combine into a basis. Over C splitting always holds; over R the displayed real conclusion needs all roots real. An arbitrary independent subset does not necessarily span: we need n independent vectors in dimension n. Generalized eigenvectors include ordinary eigenvectors, so a complete list can contain both kinds. If the root has multiplicity one, its generalized eigenspace equals its ordinary eigenspace. A scalar matrix can have a repeated root with no new directions in higher kernels: its ordinary eigenspace already fills the corresponding generalized eigenspace. Thus a repeated root does not require a genuinely higher-order vector. For the preceding J, k=2, G_2=null((J-2I)2)=R2 and {v,w} is such a basis. For the earlier diag(J,3) example F, G_2=span{e1,e2} and G_3=span{e3}. Do not claim an arbitrary generalized basis gives canonical Jordan blocks; chain-adapted choices give that additional form. Coupling may remain, so this theorem does not assert diagonalizability or the ordinary eigenmode formula.

Sources: University of Lethbridge: generalized eigenvectors and Treil: advanced spectral theory.

Similarity changes coordinates, not the operator

Matrices \(\mat{A}\) and \(\mat{B}\) are similar when

\[ \mat{B}=\mat{P}^{-1}\mat{A}\mat{P},\qquad\mat{P}\text{ invertible} \]

Their characteristic polynomials agree:

\[ \det(\lambda\mat{I}-\mat{B}) =\det\bigl(\mat{P}^{-1}(\lambda\mat{I}-\mat{A})\mat{P}\bigr) =\det(\lambda\mat{I}-\mat{A}) \]

  • Same eigenvalues, multiplicities, trace, and determinant
  • If \(\mat{B}\vct{c}=\lambda\vct{c}\), then \(\mat{A}(\mat{P}\vct{c})=\lambda\mat{P}\vct{c}\)
  • Eigenvector coordinate columns usually change

Diagonalization is similarity to a diagonal matrix; ordinary row reduction is a different operation

The identity lambda I=P^{-1}(lambda I)P gives the displayed factorization. The nonzero determinant factors of P cancel. Similarity also preserves rank and eigenspace dimensions, via the invertible coordinate change. Row operations are left multiplication by an invertible matrix; similarity requires matching inverse changes on the left and right. For example rref(diag(2,3))=I changes its eigenvalues. Equal characteristic polynomials alone do not imply similarity: S and J in the repeated-root example differ in eigenspace dimension. Avoid treating eigenvalues as a full classification.

A quarter turn has no real eigendirection

\[ \mat{R}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mat{R}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-y\\x\end{bmatrix} \]

Every nonzero real vector turns through \(90^\circ\); none stays on its line

\[ p_{\mat{R}}(\lambda)=\lambda^2+1 \]

  • Over \(\reals\): no roots, no eigenvectors, no diagonalization
  • Over \(\mathbb{C}\): roots \(i,-i\), where \(i^2=-1\)

Always name the scalar field when deciding whether an eigenvector basis exists

R maps real vectors to real vectors and has determinant 1; absence of real eigenvectors does not imply singularity. A 180-degree rotation is different: -I has every nonzero real vector as an eigenvector. On C^2 the same matrix acts complex-linearly, with two distinct eigenvalues. This is an extension of the scalar field, as in the oscillator example in Bases and Coordinates.

Complex coordinates reveal two eigenvectors

For the quarter-turn matrix, direct multiplication gives

\[ \mat{R}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-i\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}i\\1\end{bmatrix} =i\begin{bmatrix}1\\-i\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mat{R}\begin{bmatrix}1\\i\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}-i\\1\end{bmatrix} =-i\begin{bmatrix}1\\i\end{bmatrix} \]

Thus, over \(\mathbb{C}\),

\[ \mat{V}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-i&i\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mat{D}=\begin{bmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mat{R}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\mat{V}^{-1} \]

For real \(x,y\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} =\frac{x+iy}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-i\end{bmatrix} +\frac{x-iy}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\i\end{bmatrix} \]

Conjugate coefficients and eigenvectors reconstruct a real vector

The determinant of V is 2i, so V is invertible. Its inverse is (1/2)[[1,i],[1,-i]]. The coordinate formula follows by multiplication. Applying R multiplies the first coefficient by i and the second by -i; they remain conjugates, and their reconstruction is real. Do not draw the complex eigenvectors as real planar arrows. The actual real motion is still a quarter turn, not two real stretches.

Real matrices pair their nonreal eigenvalues

If \(\mat{A}\) has real entries and \(\mat{A}\vct{v}=\lambda\vct{v}\), conjugate both sides:

\[ \mat{A}\overline{\vct{v}}=\overline{\lambda}\,\overline{\vct{v}} \]

Nonreal eigenvalues and their eigenvectors occur in conjugate pairs

For \(\lambda=a+ib\), \(\vct{v}=\vct{p}+i\vct{q}\), equate real and imaginary parts:

\[ \mat{A}\vct{p}=a\vct{p}-b\vct{q},\qquad \mat{A}\vct{q}=b\vct{p}+a\vct{q} \]

When \(b\ne0\), \(\vct{p},\vct{q}\) span a real invariant plane

A complex pair describes a real two-dimensional action, even without real eigenvectors in that plane

For b nonzero, p and q are real-linearly independent. Otherwise v would be a nonzero complex scalar times a real vector w, giving A w=lambda w, impossible for a nonreal lambda and real nonzero w. In the ordered real basis {p,q}, the plane action has matrix [[a,b],[-b,a]]. This is rotation and uniform scaling in those coordinates, but p and q need not be orthonormal, so it need not be Euclidean rotation/scaling in the original coordinates. The next exercise uses a matrix already expressed in an orthonormal standard basis, where the geometric interpretation is literal.

A matrix geometric series gives an iterative solve

Powers act separately on eigenbasis coordinates

If \(\mat{A}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\mat{V}^{-1}\), cancellation gives, for integers \(k\ge0\),

\[ \mat{A}^k=\mat{V}\mat{D}^k\mat{V}^{-1},\qquad \mat{D}^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\ldots,\lambda_n^k) \]

If \(\vct{x}_0=\sum_{j=1}^n c_j\vct{v}_j\), then

\[ \vct{x}_k=\mat{A}^k\vct{x}_0 =\sum_{j=1}^n c_j\lambda_j^k\vct{v}_j \]

  • \(|\lambda_j|<1\): that component tends to zero
  • \(\lambda_j=1\): that component stays fixed
  • \(\lambda_j=-1\): that component alternates sign
  • \(|\lambda_j|>1\): a nonzero component grows in magnitude

A^0=I and D^0=I by definition, including when an eigenvalue is zero; avoid interpreting the k=0 case as a scalar 0^0 limit. For k>=1 the powers are ordinary scalar powers. Negative powers require A invertible. Complex components include phase changes; their magnitudes scale by |lambda|^k. This formula assumes an eigenvector basis. A missing initial component c_j=0 stays absent, so a large eigenvalue does not make every initial vector grow. The optional generalized-eigenvector topic introduces a coupled Jordan block; powers of defective matrices and a general Jordan algorithm are not developed here.

Functions act separately on eigenbasis coordinates

If \(\mat{A}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\mat{V}^{-1}\) and \(f\) is defined at every eigenvalue, define

\[\begin{equation*} f(\mat{A})=\mat{V}\,\operatorname{diag}\bigl(f(\lambda_1),\ldots,f(\lambda_n)\bigr)\,\mat{V}^{-1} \end{equation*}\]

Each eigenvector is scaled by the corresponding scalar value:

\[\begin{equation*} f(\mat{A})\vct{v}_j=f(\lambda_j)\vct{v}_j \end{equation*}\]

Scalar function Matrix function
\(f(z)=z^k\), integer \(k\ge0\) \(\mat{A}^k\)
\(f(z)=1/z\), no zero eigenvalue \(\mat{A}^{-1}\)
\(f(z)=1/(1-z)\), no eigenvalue \(1\) \((\mat{I}-\mat{A})^{-1}\)
\(f(z)=e^{tz}\) \(e^{t\mat{A}}\)

Apply \(f\) to the eigenvalues, then change back — not to the individual matrix entries

This definition assumes a complete eigenvector basis over the chosen scalar field, and f must be defined at all eigenvalues. It is independent of the eigenvector ordering, scaling and choice of basis within a repeated eigenspace. For a polynomial p, cancellation in powers gives the usual p(A). A convergent power series similarly agrees with this formula. Do not claim this prescription defines arbitrary matrix functions for defective matrices; the exponential on the next slide is defined for all square matrices by its convergent series. For k=0, use the constant function f(z)=1, including at zero. The companion checks p(A)=A^2-2A for the running example.

Taylor series give more matrix functions

For \(\mat{A}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\mat{V}^{-1}\), let the Taylor series \(f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_kz^k\) converge at every eigenvalue

\[\begin{equation*} f(\mat{A})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_k\mat{A}^k =\mat{V}\operatorname{diag}\bigl(f(\lambda_1),\ldots,f(\lambda_n)\bigr)\mat{V}^{-1} \end{equation*}\]

For example,

\[\begin{align*} \sin(\mat{A})&=\mat{A}-\frac{\mat{A}^3}{3!}+\frac{\mat{A}^5}{5!}-\cdots\\ \cos(\mat{A})&=\mat{I}-\frac{\mat{A}^2}{2!}+\frac{\mat{A}^4}{4!}-\cdots \end{align*}\]

The same series coefficients; matrix powers replace scalar powers

For a diagonalizable matrix, convergence at every eigenvalue gives convergence of the matrix series. For a Taylor series centered at a, replace scalar powers (z-a)^k by (A-aI)^k. The sine and cosine series converge for every square matrix, even without diagonalizability. These are matrix series, not entrywise sine and cosine. The exponential is the next example.

The matrix exponential solves a linear differential system

For any square matrix, define the matrix exponential

\[\begin{equation*} e^{t\mat{A}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^k\mat{A}^k}{k!} =\mat{I}+t\mat{A}+\frac{t^2}{2}\mat{A}^2+\cdots \end{equation*}\]

For a constant matrix, the linear differential system has the unique solution

\[\begin{equation*} \vct{x}'=\mat{A}\vct{x},\quad\vct{x}(0)=\vct{x}_0 \qquad\implies\qquad \vct{x}(t)=e^{t\mat{A}}\vct{x}_0 \end{equation*}\]

If \(\mat{A}\) has an eigenvector basis,

\[\begin{equation*} e^{t\mat{A}}=\mat{V}e^{t\mat{D}}\mat{V}^{-1},\qquad e^{t\mat{D}}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{t\lambda_1},\ldots,e^{t\lambda_n}) \end{equation*}\]

The matrix exponential works for every square matrix; the diagonal formula needs an eigenbasis

A is constant and t is real. The series converges for every finite square A and every finite t. Termwise differentiation gives E’(t)=A E(t) and E(0)=I, so E(t)x0 solves the initial-value problem, even if A is not diagonalizable. Uniqueness follows, for example, by differentiating e^(-tA)x(t): the product is constant and equals x0. The derivative identity uses that A commutes with its exponential. Only the eigenbasis formulas require diagonalizability. Real parts of eigenvalues determine exponential growth/decay for complex modes; no general stability theory is required here. The identity at t=0 is I, not a matrix of entrywise exponentials. Unlike A itself, e^(tA) is always invertible, with inverse e^(-tA). A zero eigenvalue gives a constant ODE mode, not a collapsed solution: e^(t*0)=1. In discrete steps lambda=1 is fixed; in continuous time lambda=0 is an equilibrium direction. Avoid interchanging multipliers and rates.

Eigenbasis coordinates decouple a differential system

Let \(\mat{A}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\mat{V}^{-1}\) and write \(\vct{x}(t)=\mat{V}\vct{c}(t)\)

The basis matrix is constant, so

\[\begin{equation*} \vct{x}'=\mat{A}\vct{x} \iff\mat{V}\vct{c}'=\mat{A}\mat{V}\vct{c}=\mat{V}\mat{D}\vct{c} \iff\boxed{\vct{c}'=\mat{D}\vct{c}} \end{equation*}\]

Each coordinate solves its own scalar equation:

\[\begin{equation*} c_j'(t)=\lambda_jc_j(t) \quad\implies\quad c_j(t)=e^{\lambda_jt}c_j(0) \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \boxed{\vct{x}(t)=\mat{V}e^{t\mat{D}}\mat{V}^{-1}\vct{x}_0} \end{equation*}\]

\[\begin{equation*} \vct{x}(t) \xleftarrow{\ \mat{V}\ } \vct{c}(t) \xleftarrow{\ e^{t\mat{D}}\ } \underbrace{\vct{c}_0}_{\text{initial coordinates}} \xleftarrow{\ \mat{V}^{-1}\ } \underbrace{\vct{x}_0}_{\text{initial state}} \end{equation*}\]

Read right to left: change to the eigenbasis, evolve each coordinate, then reconstruct

Both the state and its velocity use the same constant basis. A time-dependent basis would contribute a V’(t)c(t) term and is not under discussion here. Define c0=c(0)=V^{-1}x0. The evolving c_j(t) is not a constant; its initial value c_j(0) is the fixed coefficient of the solution mode. For a complex eigenbasis of a real matrix, conjugate modes reconstruct real solutions. Read both the boxed product and the arrow line from right to left: V^{-1}x0 gives the initial eigenbasis coordinates, e^(tD) evolves them, and V reconstructs the current state. The factor x0 is essential for a solution vector. Without x0, V e^(tD) V{-1}=e(tA) is the evolution matrix, not the state vector.

Initial data choose a whole solution

Use \(\mathbb{K}=\reals\) or \(\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}\); \(\mathbb{K}^n\) means columns of \(n\) real or complex numbers

For a constant \(\mat{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{n\times n}\), let

\[\begin{equation*} S_{\mat{A}}=\{\vct{x}:\reals\to\mathbb{K}^n:\vct{x}'=\mat{A}\vct{x}\} \end{equation*}\]

The solution map \(T_{\mat{A}}:\mathbb{K}^n\to S_{\mat{A}}\) builds the whole function

Initial state in \(\mathbb{K}^n\) \(\vct{x}_0=\begin{bmatrix}x_1(0)\\\vdots\\x_n(0)\end{bmatrix}\)

\(\xleftarrow{\quad T_{\mat{A}}\quad}\)

Whole solution in \(S_{\mat{A}}\) \(\vct{x}(\cdot):t\longmapsto e^{t\mat{A}}\vct{x}_0\)

Evaluation at one time: \(\operatorname{ev}_t\circ T_{\mat{A}}=e^{t\mat{A}}\)

\(T_{\mat{A}}\) is a linear isomorphism, with inverse \(\vct{x}\mapsto\vct{x}(0)\)

\(\dim S_{\mat{A}}=n\), even when \(\mat{A}\) is not diagonalizable

S_A consists of continuously differentiable functions on all real t; the constant-coefficient equation makes them smooth. Addition and scalar multiplication are pointwise. Closure follows by differentiating linear combinations. T_A is linear because e^(tA) is linear at every t. Existence and uniqueness give a bijection: every initial vector gives exactly one solution, and evaluating that solution at zero returns the vector. T_A is a map into a function space, not the numerical matrix e^(tA) for one chosen t. This generalizes the earlier initial-data-to-spring-solution map in Bases and Coordinates. The zero vector of S_A is the zero function. Blackboard-bold K denotes specifically R or C in this differential-equation context; its nth power is the space of n-component columns. Choose one scalar field consistently for both boxes.

An eigenbasis becomes a basis of solution functions

If \(\mathcal{V}=\{\vct{v}_1,\ldots,\vct{v}_n\}\) is an eigenvector basis, define the solution modes

\[\begin{equation*} \vct{\phi}_j=T_{\mat{A}}(\vct{v}_j),\qquad \vct{\phi}_j(t)=e^{\lambda_jt}\vct{v}_j \end{equation*}\]

The isomorphism carries a basis to a basis: \(\mathcal{F}=\{\vct{\phi}_1,\ldots,\vct{\phi}_n\}\) of \(S_{\mat{A}}\)

Initial vector \(\displaystyle\vct{x}_0=\sum_{j=1}^n c_j(0)\vct{v}_j\) \([\vct{x}_0]_{\mathcal{V}}=\begin{bmatrix}c_1(0)\\\vdots\\c_n(0)\end{bmatrix}\)

\(\xleftarrow{\quad T_{\mat{A}}\quad}\)

Whole solution function \(\displaystyle\vct{x}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^n c_j(0)\vct{\phi}_j\) \([\vct{x}(\cdot)]_{\mathcal{F}}=\begin{bmatrix}c_1(0)\\\vdots\\c_n(0)\end{bmatrix}\)

Independence can be seen without a new theorem: evaluate a zero combination of phi_j at zero to obtain the same zero combination of v_j, forcing all coefficients to vanish. The basis vectors of S_A are whole vector-valued functions, not their values at one t. The coordinates of that whole function are constant c_j(0); the coordinates of its current state are c_j(t). Differentiation has the same eigenvalues on the solution space

On \(S_{\mat{A}}\), differentiation is a linear operator \(\mathcal{L}:\vct{x}\mapsto\vct{x}'\)

Each solution mode is an eigenfunction of differentiation:

\[\begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\vct{\phi}_j =\vct{\phi}_j' =\lambda_j\vct{\phi}_j \end{equation*}\]

The solution map connects the two actions:

\[\begin{equation*} \boxed{\mathcal{L}\circ T_{\mat{A}}=T_{\mat{A}}\circ\mat{A}} \qquad [\mathcal{L}]_{\mathcal{F}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}}=\mat{D} \end{equation*}\]

In state space: eigenvectors of a matrix

In solution space: eigenvectors are exponential solution functions

This is a conceptual extension connecting abstract linear operators and eigenvectors; it may be skipped on a first pass. Because A is constant, if x’=Ax, then (x’)‘=A x’, so differentiation maps S_A into itself. For any u, (T_A u)’(t)=A e(tA)u=e(tA)Au=(T_A(Au))(t). Thus L=T_A A T_A^{-1}, a similarity of operators on different spaces. The matrix D represents A in the eigenbasis V and differentiation in the function basis F. Applying A to initial data is distinct from advancing time; time advancement is e^(tA). No spectrum of an infinite-dimensional function space is needed: S_A has dimension n.

Symmetry makes different eigenspaces perpendicular

Let \(\mat{A}=\mat{A}^\top\) be real, with \(\mat{A}\vct{u}=\lambda\vct{u}\) and \(\mat{A}\vct{v}=\mu\vct{v}\)

Symmetry lets the action move across the dot product:

\[ \lambda\vct{u}^\top\vct{v} =(\mat{A}\vct{u})^\top\vct{v} =\vct{u}^\top\mat{A}\vct{v} =\mu\vct{u}^\top\vct{v} \]

Thus

\[ (\lambda-\mu)\vct{u}^\top\vct{v}=0 \]

Distinct eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix have perpendicular eigenvectors

 

Within a repeated eigenspace, perpendicular basis vectors must still be chosen

The displayed calculation concerns real eigenvectors and eigenvalues. The spectral theorem on the next slide supplies their existence for all real symmetric matrices. For a general nonsymmetric matrix, distinct eigenvalues still imply independence but do not imply perpendicular eigenvectors: B in the earlier exercise has eigenvectors [1,0]^T and [1,-2]^T. In a repeated eigenspace every linear combination remains an eigenvector; therefore Gram-Schmidt within that eigenspace, introduced in Inner Products, can choose an orthonormal basis without changing the eigenvalue.

The spectral theorem supplies an orthonormal eigenbasis

Spectral theorem: every real symmetric matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable

It has a real orthonormal eigenbasis

Put its unit eigenvectors in \(\mat{Q}\), in the same order as \(\mat{D}\):

\[ \mat{Q}^\top\mat{Q}=\mat{I},\qquad \mat{Q}^{-1}=\mat{Q}^\top,\qquad \boxed{\mat{A}=\mat{Q}\mat{D}\mat{Q}^\top} \]

  • All eigenvalues are real; repeated eigenvalues are allowed
  • Coordinates are dot products: \([\vct{x}]_{\mathcal{Q}}=\mat{Q}^\top\vct{x}\)
  • Euclidean lengths are preserved: \(\|\mat{Q}^\top\vct{x}\|=\|\vct{x}\|\)

A real matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable exactly when it is symmetric

State the existence part as a theorem; the preceding orthogonality calculation alone does not prove it. One proof selects a real unit eigenvector by maximizing x^T A x on the unit sphere, shows its perpendicular complement is invariant, and uses induction. That proof needs tools beyond the current course prerequisites and is not required here. Conversely, Q D Q^T with real diagonal D is symmetric by taking its transpose. Length preservation follows by squaring the norm and using QQ^T=I. This strengthens the general coordinate isomorphism, which preserves algebra but need not preserve ordinary coordinate lengths. Repeated eigenspaces admit orthonormal bases.
